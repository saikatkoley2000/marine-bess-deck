Case Study & Root Cause

Case Study: The Ytterøyningen Ferry Fire (2019)

⚠️ The Incident Sequence

  • Vessel: Norled hybrid ferry MF Ytterøyningen equipped with a ~2 MWh liquid-cooled ESS.
  • Initial Event (10 Oct 2019): Fire alarm triggered in battery room while berthed. Pack BMS had been disconnected for routine maintenance.
  • Suppression Actions: Novec 1230 gas and brief activation of the ship's saltwater deluge sprinkler system.
  • Catastrophic Explosion (11 Oct 2019): ~12 hours later, a massive hydrogen/syngas explosion blew open doors and warped structural bulkheads.

🔍 Official Findings (Corvus & NMA)

  • Primary Cause: A twisted gasket inside the water-glycol cold-plate circuit leaked conductive coolant onto energized components.
  • Electrical Arcing: Conductive fluid bridged live parts at 1,000 V DC, creating high-energy sustained arcing.
  • Fuel Source: The combustible ethylene glycol liquid in the coolant mixture fueled and sustained the fire.
  • Sprinkler Escalation: The Norwegian Maritime Authority found seawater sprinkler discharge caused further short circuits across the high-voltage string.
The Indisputable Lesson for Class Societies: Water-glycol cooling brings both electrical conductivity and combustible fuel directly inside the high-voltage pack. A dielectric fluid completely removes this hazard by design.
Engineering Architecture

Cooling Architectural Comparison

1. Forced Air

Corvus Orca, EST Octopus

  • Heat Transfer: Low convective efficiency; large thermal gradients across modules.
  • Derating: Requires severe C-rate throttles in tropical ambient conditions (>35°C).
  • Environmental Risk: Ingress of humid, saline maritime air induces salt fog corrosion.
  • Propagation: Relies entirely on passive solid barriers (aerogel/mica).

2. Cold-Plate (Water/Glycol)

Leclanché MRS-3, Siemens BlueVault

  • Heat Transfer: High at cold-plate contact face, but leaves internal thermal gradients.
  • Leak Hazard: Conductive fluid leak causes 1,000 V DC arcing (as on Ytterøyningen).
  • Fuel Contamination: Ethylene glycol adds combustible load to the pack interior.
  • Class Mandate: DNV 2023 rules enforce strict leak detection and fault current mitigation.

3. Dielectric Immersion

Synthetic Ester Architecture

  • Heat Transfer: Direct 100% surface contact on all cell walls and busbars.
  • Leak Hazard: Fluid is purely dielectric (>50 kV breakdown). Leak cannot trigger arcing.
  • Fire Protection: Ultra-high fire point (>300°C K3 class). Acts as an active thermal snuffer.
  • Propagation: Quenches thermal spikes Sensible + Latent before runaway spreads.

The Thermal Physics of Quenching

Under abusive conditions (internal micro-short, mechanical puncture, or localized heating):

  • Immersion Heat Sink: The surrounding bath of fluid instantly absorbs sensible and latent heat directly at the localized cell surface.

  • Oxygen Exclusion: The dense fluid blanket completely isolates venting cell gases from atmospheric oxygen at the point of release.

Defensible Answers to Key Class Inquiries

Q1: Fluid Fire Load in the Battery Room?

Answer: The fluid is a K3-class synthetic ester with an auto-ignition temperature >400°C and fire point >300°C. Operating at <55°C, it presents zero vapor pressure and cannot be ignited by 1,000 V DC arcs. It serves as a fire barrier, not a fire accelerant.

Q2: Off-Gas Routing Through Immersion Liquid?

Answer: Vent gases bubble rapidly through the dielectric bath (which actively cools the gas and condenses heavy aerosols) into a sealed headspace, which exhausts through a calibrated non-return burst valve directly to weather deck.

Q3: Fluid Sloshing at 45° Sea Inclination?

Answer: Enclosures feature internal flow baffles and positive-pressure expansion bladders. Cells remain 100% submerged and wetted across all static and dynamic roll/pitch angles compliant with IACS UR E10.

Q4: Fixed Extinguishment System Compatibility?

Answer: The immersion tank is a sealed primary pressure boundary. External space protection (e.g. Novec 1230, aerosol, or water mist) acts on the battery room boundaries without contaminating the dielectric fluid.

Case Study & Lessons Learned: The Ytterøyningen Incident (2019)

Detailed chronology, Norwegian Maritime Authority findings, and regulatory reforms

⚠️ Incident Chronology (10–11 October 2019)

Vessel Profile: Norled hybrid diesel-electric passenger/car ferry MF Ytterøyningen equipped with a ~2 MWh Corvus liquid-cooled lithium-ion ESS.

  • 10 October 2019 (Evening): A battery room fire alarm sounded while the ferry was berthed at Sydnes. The vessel's BMS had been disconnected for scheduled maintenance.
  • Initial Firefighting: The crew evacuated the ship; the fixed Novec 1230 gas suppression system discharged, and the ship's seawater sprinkler system was briefly activated.
  • 11 October 2019 (~07:00 AM): Approximately 12 hours after the initial fire was thought extinguished, a violent syngas/hydrogen explosion occurred in the battery room, tearing off steel doors and hospitalizing 12 firefighters for smoke/gas inhalation.

🔍 Root Cause & Technical Findings

  • Twisted Gasket Leak: Corvus Energy's preliminary findings (December 2019, verified by the Norwegian Maritime Authority) traced the origin to a mechanical leak from a twisted gasket in the liquid cold-plate circuit.
  • High-Voltage DC Arcing: The conductive water-glycol coolant dripped across energized components, creating sustained electrical arcing at 1,000 V DC pack voltages.
  • Ethylene Glycol as Fuel: The ethylene glycol contained in the coolant mixture acted as an active combustible hydrocarbon fuel that ignited and sustained the fire.
  • Seawater Sprinkler Escalation: The Norwegian Maritime Authority (12 Dec 2019) determined that brief seawater deluge caused widespread electrical short circuits across battery strings, intensifying flammable off-gas accumulation.

🛡️ Why Dielectric Immersion Prevents This Failure Mode by Design

1. Non-Conductive Fluid

Dielectric breakdown strength >50 kV prevents electrical bridging. A leak cannot create high-voltage DC arcing or short-circuit faults.

2. Fire Point >300°C

K3-class synthetic ester cannot ignite at pack operating temperatures and does not provide volatile hydrocarbon fuel like ethylene glycol.

3. DNV 2023 Compliance

Directly satisfies DNV July 2023 rules addressing "leakages from cooling systems that may cause fault currents".

Q1: Does the dielectric fluid increase battery room fire load?

Engineering Justification: The fluid is an IEC 61100 K3-class synthetic ester with a fire point >300°C and flash point >260°C. Normal battery operating temperatures are ≤55°C. Unlike water-glycol, the fluid has zero electrical conductivity and cannot be ignited by 1,000 V DC arcing. It serves as an active flame-quenching medium rather than a combustible hazard.

Q2: How is off-gas collected if bubbling through the fluid?

Engineering Justification: Vent gases rise rapidly through the dielectric bath (which actively cools the gases and condenses flammable liquid aerosols) into an engineered expansion headspace. From the headspace, gases are exhausted through calibrated non-return burst valves directly into dedicated steel exhaust piping leading to the open weather deck.

Q3: How is fluid level maintained during ship roll and pitch?

Engineering Justification: The containment enclosure is fitted with internal anti-slosh baffle plates and a closed expansion volume. Cells remain 100% submerged and wetted across static inclinations of 22.5° and dynamic roll/pitch angles of 45° under IACS UR E10. Redundant ultrasonic level sensors alarm on fluid loss.

Q4: How does fixed fire extinguishing interact with fluid tanks?

Engineering Justification: The immersion tank is a sealed primary pressure boundary. Fixed fire-extinguishing agents (water mist, aerosol, or Novec 1230) protect the external compartment boundaries without penetrating or contaminating the dielectric fluid.
